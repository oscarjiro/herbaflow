A thesis 5 min read

A pipeline you can argue with.

Herbaflow began as a thesis project, frustrated by the dozen browser tabs it took to trace a single herbal compound back to the diseases it might treat. This is a note about why it exists, and what it cannot yet do.
Author A solo computational biology thesis
Field Network pharmacology · in silico screening
Status v1.0 · undergraduate thesis
Last revised 14 March 2026

Network pharmacology is, depending on who you ask, either a beautifully integrative view of how a plant's many small molecules act on many protein targets at once, or a sprawl of unrelated databases held together by spreadsheets. Both are true. The work of getting from a herbal source — Curcuma longa, say, or Salvia miltiorrhiza — to a defensible hypothesis about which diseases it might treat is mostly a matter of stitching these databases together, by hand, while keeping track of which assumptions you made along the way.

Herbaflow tries to do that stitching for you. But not invisibly. The product sits on a single principle: reviewability beats automation. Every association — every edge in the eventual compound‑target‑disease graph — is annotated with its source database, the parameters used, and the cut‑offs applied. You can override any of them, and the system will tell you what your override changed downstream.

Why this, why now

The conventional pipeline looks like this: pull metabolite lists from TCMSP, filter by oral bioavailability and drug‑likeness, query STITCH or SwissTargetPrediction for protein targets, cluster the result, look up disease associations in DisGeNET, draw a network. Every step has a tool. None of them talk to each other. Most of the published work is a brief paragraph in the methods section that says, in effect, we did the thing.

For wet‑lab researchers — the people best placed to validate any in‑silico hypothesis — this is a wall. Not a technical one exactly: it is a coordination problem. The cost of doing network pharmacology correctly is the cost of running the analysis and writing the code to keep its provenance straight. Herbaflow folds the second cost into the first.

What it is not

This is a thesis project, not a platform. The database snapshots are static — I cannot afford to keep them live — and refreshing them costs the indexing time we surface on every screen. There is no molecular docking automation; for now the docking step exposes a deliberate empty state with manual export instructions and a note about why automation is out of scope.

“A pipeline that hides its assumptions is not faster than one that shows them. It is only quieter.” — from the thesis introduction

What we built instead

The product is organised as a sequence of inspectable steps. Each one produces an intermediate set — metabolites, then targets, then clusters, then diseases — and each set is rendered as both a table you can edit and a visualisation you can read. You can leave reviewer notes on any node or edge, and they travel with the final export.

We also kept the interface deliberately quiet. There are no streaks, no celebratory toasts, no progress gamification. The product treats its users as researchers who will judge it by the quality of its citations, not the cleverness of its UI.

A note on coverage

At the time of writing, the database includes 1,247 plants, ~38k metabolites, ~14k human protein targets, and 12k disease associations. The plants come primarily from East Asian and South Asian materia medica; coverage of African and South American traditions is, frankly, poor. Expanding this is part of the open work.

¹ On reviewability. Every screen shows the parameters that produced what you see, and lets you revert, replay, or fork them.
² Sources used. TCMSP, BATMAN-TCM, TCMID, STITCH, SwissTargetPrediction, DisGeNET, OMIM, TTD. Versions and indexing dates are stamped on every export.
³ Not yet automated. Molecular docking, structural validation, and pharmacokinetic modelling. These require licensed tooling beyond the scope of a thesis.

The goal was never to replace the researcher.
It was to make sure the pipeline could be read aloud.

Most network‑pharmacology figures in the literature come with a one‑sentence methods note. Herbaflow's exports come with the parameters, the database versions, and the manual overrides — a full audit trail for the methods section.

Known limitations

What we ship, what we don't.

The limits below are the honest perimeter of a thesis project. They are surfaced in the product itself — not buried in release notes.

L · 01

Static reference databases

Plants, metabolites, targets, and diseases are pinned to dated snapshots. The UI shows the index date on every result so you can judge coverage.

indexed · 14 mar 2026
L · 02

No automated docking

The docking step exports the candidate set to a format ready for AutoDock Vina. We do not run docking ourselves yet — pretending to would be worse than admitting we don't.

manual export only
L · 03

Coverage skews regional

Source databases over‑represent East and South Asian traditional medicine. African and South American materia medica are sparsely covered. Expansion is open work.

37% non-Asian sources
